Reduced rank linear regression

5/25/2026; edited 8/30/2026

Recently, I have been interested in reduced-rank linear regression, an idea that goes back to Anderson (1951) and Izenman (1975). My first stop was section 3.7, “Multiple Outcome Shrinkage and Selection,” of The Elements of Statistical Learning. I found the treatment there a bit unclear, and others have felt the same way, so I decided to derive the solution by hand as a fun exercise.

Derivation

Suppose we have X∈Rn×pX\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}X∈Rn×p, Y∈Rn×qY\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times q}Y∈Rn×q, and positive integer rank rrr (typically r<n,p,qr \lt n, p, qr<n,p,q). We want to find a matrix Θ∈Rp×q\Theta \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times q}Θ∈Rp×q that solves the problem

min⁡Θ.∥Y−XΘ∥F2s.t.rank(Θ)≤r. \begin{align} \min_{\Theta}. \quad & \|Y - X\Theta\|_F^2 \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \text{rank}(\Theta) \leq r.\nonumber \end{align} Θmin​.s.t.​∥Y−XΘ∥F2​rank(Θ)≤r.​​

This problem looks benign, but the rank constraint is non-convex, so it is not immediately clear how to solve it. Some simple manipulations yield a two-step closed-form solution, up to numerical precision.

First, let Θ^\hat{\Theta}Θ^ be the ordinary least squares solution to the unconstrained problem min⁡Θ.∥Y−XΘ∥F2\min_{\Theta}. \|Y - X\Theta\|_F^2minΘ​.∥Y−XΘ∥F2​. The normal equations of the unconstrained problem give us that

XT(XΘ^−Y)=0. X^T(X\hat{\Theta} - Y) = 0. XT(XΘ^−Y)=0.

If we define the residual matrix R=Y−XΘ^R = Y - X\hat{\Theta}R=Y−XΘ^, then XTR=0X^TR = 0XTR=0. It follows that for all Θ∈Rp×q\Theta\in \mathbb{R}^{p\times q}Θ∈Rp×q, we have

∥Y−XΘ∥F2=∥R+XΘ^−XΘ∥F2=∥R+X(Θ^−Θ)∥F2=∥R∥F2+∥XΘ^−XΘ∥F2+2tr(RTX(Θ^−Θ))=∥R∥F2+∥XΘ^−XΘ∥F2, \begin{align*} \|Y - X\Theta\|_F^2 & = \|R + X\hat{\Theta} - X\Theta\|_F^2 \\ & = \|R + X(\hat{\Theta} - \Theta)\|_F^2 \\ & = \|R\|_F^2 + \|X \hat{\Theta} - X\Theta\|_F^2 + 2\mathrm{tr}\left(R^TX(\hat{\Theta} - \Theta)\right) \\ & = \|R\|_F^2 + \|X \hat{\Theta} - X\Theta\|_F^2, \end{align*} ∥Y−XΘ∥F2​​=∥R+XΘ^−XΘ∥F2​=∥R+X(Θ^−Θ)∥F2​=∥R∥F2​+∥XΘ^−XΘ∥F2​+2tr(RTX(Θ^−Θ))=∥R∥F2​+∥XΘ^−XΘ∥F2​,​

where the cross term drops out because RTX=(XTR)T=0R^TX = \left(X^TR\right)^T = 0RTX=(XTR)T=0. Since RRR has no dependence on Θ\ThetaΘ, the problem (1) is equivalent to

min⁡Θ.∥XΘ^−XΘ∥F2s.t.rank(Θ)≤r. \begin{align} \min_{\Theta}. \quad & \|X \hat{\Theta} - X\Theta\|_F^2 \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \text{rank}(\Theta) \leq r.\nonumber \end{align} Θmin​.s.t.​∥XΘ^−XΘ∥F2​rank(Θ)≤r.​​

Now, consider the following relaxation of problem (2).

min⁡M.∥XΘ^−M∥F2s.t.rank(M)≤r, \begin{align} \min_{M}. \quad & \|X \hat{\Theta} - M\|_F^2 \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \text{rank}(M) \leq r,\nonumber \end{align} Mmin​.s.t.​∥XΘ^−M∥F2​rank(M)≤r,​​

where M∈Rn×qM\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times q}M∈Rn×q is a new variable. This is a relaxation because any feasible Θ\ThetaΘ for problem (2) gives a feasible M=XΘM = X\ThetaM=XΘ for problem (3) with the same objective value, so the optimal value of (3) is at most the optimal value of (2). We will show that the two optimal values are in fact equal, and that a solution to problem (3) hands us a solution to problem (2).

The solution to (3) is the best rank-rrr (or less) approximation to XΘ^X\hat{\Theta}XΘ^ in the Frobenius norm. If we define XΘ^=UΣVTX\hat{\Theta} = U\Sigma V^TXΘ^=UΣVT to be the thin SVD of XΘ^X\hat{\Theta}XΘ^, then by the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem, this approximation is given by U:rΣ:rV:rTU_{:r}\Sigma_{:r} V_{:r}^TU:r​Σ:r​V:rT​, where U:rU_{:r}U:r​ and V:rV_{:r}V:r​ are the first rrr columns of UUU and VVV, and Σ:r\Sigma_{:r}Σ:r​ is the leading r×rr\times rr×r block of Σ\SigmaΣ. Thus, we have that

M⋆=U:rΣ:rV:rT=UΣVTV:rV:rT=XΘ^V:rV:rT. \begin{align*} M^\star & = U_{:r}\Sigma_{:r} V_{:r}^T \\ & = U\Sigma V^T V_{:r}V_{:r}^T \\ & = X\hat{\Theta} V_{:r} V_{:r}^T. \end{align*} M⋆​=U:r​Σ:r​V:rT​=UΣVTV:r​V:rT​=XΘ^V:r​V:rT​.​

Since XΘ^V:rV:rTX\hat{\Theta} V_{:r} V_{:r}^TXΘ^V:r​V:rT​ is a solution to the relaxation (3), it follows that Θ^V:rV:rT\hat{\Theta} V_{:r} V_{:r}^TΘ^V:r​V:rT​ is a solution to the original problem (2).

The core intuition is that the least squares residual is orthogonal to the column space of XXX. Choosing the best rank-rrr coefficient matrix Θ\ThetaΘ amounts to choosing the best subspace within that column space. The two residuals are therefore orthogonal, and the problem splits into two steps: find the least squares solution, then find the best rank-rrr approximation to the fitted values XΘ^X\hat{\Theta}XΘ^.

Afterthoughts

Suppose XXX and YYY have orthogonal low-rank signal but are corrupted by noise, so they appear full rank. The least squares fit Θ^\hat{\Theta}Θ^ will then be full rank, with XΘ^X\hat{\Theta}XΘ^ matching or nearly matching YYY. Reduced-rank regression might seem like a good choice here. But when XΘ^=YX\hat{\Theta}=YXΘ^=Y, the reduced-rank approximation simply picks out the leading left singular vectors of YYY from the column space of XXX, which the noise has artificially enlarged. The resulting fit will appear much better than it is. Reduced-rank regression does not act as a regularizer here, and the singular values of Θ^\hat{\Theta}Θ^ can be quite large when the model fits noise.

When XXX and YYY are noisy, it might also be tempting to compute the rank-rrr approximation of Θ^\hat{\Theta}Θ^ directly. This is even worse. When Θ^\hat{\Theta}Θ^ fits noise in XXX, its corresponding singular values will be large, so the rank-rrr approximation will select precisely those components.

In contrast, taking a low-rank approximation of XXX before doing least squares is much more like regularization. Ideally, the approximation removes the noise in XXX, forcing Θ\ThetaΘ to fit its signal instead. Reduced-rank regression on XΘ^X\hat{\Theta}XΘ^ is then more reasonable because it no longer risks selecting noise from XXX.

The more direct fix is to ask for regularization outright. I take that up in a follow-up note, where a ridge penalty joins the rank constraint. The two-step method survives, as long as we stack XXX on top of λI\sqrt{\lambda}Iλ​I before taking the SVD.

The derivation also reminded me of the orthogonal Procrustes problem. It asks for the orthogonal matrix QQQ that best maps a matrix AAA to a matrix BBB in the Frobenius norm. The solution is Q=UVTQ = UV^TQ=UVT, where UΣVTU\Sigma V^TUΣVT is the thin SVD of BATBA^TBAT, as described here. Both problems are least squares with a non-convex constraint, yet both have closed-form solutions based on the SVD.

It is worth appreciating how unusual that is. The obvious vector analogue of problem (1) is least squares subject to ∥β∥0≤k\|\beta\|_0\leq k∥β∥0​≤k, which is best subset selection, and that problem is NP-hard. The two constraints look alike from a distance, and projecting onto either set is a one-liner. Truncate the SVD in one case, and keep the kkk largest entries in the other.

The difference appears in the relaxation. Our relaxed solution was

M⋆=XΘ^V:rV:rT=X(Θ^V:rV:rT). M^\star = X\hat{\Theta}V_{:r}V_{:r}^T = X\left(\hat{\Theta}V_{:r}V_{:r}^T\right). M⋆=XΘ^V:r​V:rT​=X(Θ^V:r​V:rT​).

The truncation acts on the right, in target space, while XXX acts on the left. The two never interfere, so the relaxation is tight. Sparsity has no such structure. The best kkk-sparse approximation to the fitted values Xβ^X\hat{\beta}Xβ^​ is generally not XβX\betaXβ for any kkk-sparse β\betaβ. Thresholding and the design act on the same indices, and the design mixes them. More generally, invertible maps on either side of a matrix preserve its rank. A support set, by contrast, survives only permutations and diagonal scalings. This is why best subset selection is easy when XTXX^TXXTX is diagonal, where the problem separates coordinate by coordinate, and hard as soon as it is not.

Extra structure doesn't necessarily rescue the sparse problem either. Adding a nonnegativity constraint leaves it NP-hard.
